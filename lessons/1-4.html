Strategy 4 · Unit 1

Listen for examples and supporting details

Tell the difference between a main point and the examples that support it.

What is it?

Speakers make a general point and then support it with examples, stories, numbers or reasons.

Why does it help?

Examples help you understand and remember the main point. If you know something is an example, you don't need to write every detail.

How to do it

  1. Listen for example signals: "for example", "like", "imagine", "when I…".
  2. Ask: What general point does this example support?
  3. In your notes, write the main point first. Then indent the example under it.

Listen for

Example signals
  • For example,…
  • For instance,…
  • like…
  • such as…
  • Imagine…
  • Take…
Story signals
  • I remember…
  • One day…
  • I was in…
  • When I was…
Back to the point
  • So,…
  • That's why…
  • This shows that…
  • The thing is,…

Tip: After a story, the speaker usually says what it means. Listen hard right after the story ends.

Also use: S2 Listen for the main idea (gist) · S3 Recognize signpost language

Now can it be right to reject a student on linguistic ability alone? Perhaps you have a computer scientist who's a genius. Would he need the same language as a lawyer, for example? … Now let me put it this way: if I met a monolingual Dutch speaker who had the cure for cancer, would I stop him from entering my British University?

Point: it is wrong to reject a student only because of language. Example 1 ("Perhaps you have…"): a genius computer scientist. Example 2 ("let me put it this way: if I met…"): a Dutch speaker with a cure for cancer. In notes, write the point, then indent the examples under it.

Exam practice: beyond the words

On the final exam, many questions ask what a speaker means or why they say something. The answer is not in the words alone: think about what comes before and after.

Watch out for these wrong answers: ideas the speaker did not say; answers that are too strong (“all”, “always”, “only”); the opposite of the speaker's view; and answers with the right words but the wrong meaning.

Show a model summary

Patricia Ryan, an English teacher in the Gulf, argues that English should not be used as a barrier to education. She points out that languages are dying quickly, and that English tests reject talented people, such as a scientist who is a genius but weak in English. She suggests that translation and cooperation between languages help us learn more, as when a German scientist solved a problem for two English scientists. She concludes that we should celebrate linguistic diversity.
