Strategy 11 · Unit 2

Separate fact from opinion

Decide whether the speaker is reporting evidence or giving their own view, and how certain they are.

What is it?

Speakers mix facts (things that can be checked: research, numbers, events) with opinions (beliefs, judgments, recommendations). Hedging words show how certain they are.

Why does it help?

Critical listeners don't accept everything they hear. You need to know what is proven, what is suggested, and what is just the speaker's belief.

How to do it

  1. Ask: Could this be checked with evidence? If yes, it is probably a fact or a claim about a fact.
  2. Listen for opinion signals: "I think", "I believe", "should".
  3. Listen for hedges ("may", "might", "suggests") and boosters ("clearly", "proves").
  4. Mark your notes: F = fact, O = opinion.

Listen for

Opinion signals
  • I think / I believe…
  • In my view,…
  • We should / must…
  • It's wrong to…
  • I worry that…
Hedges (less sure)
  • may / might / could
  • suggests
  • it seems
  • probably
  • some people
Boosters (very sure)
  • clearly
  • proves
  • definitely
  • always / never
  • the fact is

Tip: A strong opinion can be based on facts. The question is whether the speaker gives the evidence.

Show a model summary

Elizabeth Loftus argues that memory is not like a recording but is constructive, so it can be changed. For example, her research found that asking whether cars "smashed" into each other made witnesses remember broken glass that wasn't there, and that false memories, such as being lost in a mall, could be planted in about a quarter of people. Real cases show that faulty eyewitness memory has led to innocent people being convicted. In her opinion, we need independent evidence before we trust a memory, and she even suggests that false memories could be used to help children eat healthily.

100 words. Your summary can be different. Compare the content, not the exact words.
