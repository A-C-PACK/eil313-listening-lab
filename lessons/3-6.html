Strategy 16 · Unit 3

Evaluate the argument and the speaker's purpose

Decide why the speaker is speaking, what they want you to believe or do, and whether the evidence supports it.

What is it?

Every talk has a purpose: to inform, to persuade or to inspire. Evaluating an argument means asking whether the reasons and evidence really support the speaker's claim.

Why does it help?

University students are expected to respond critically, not just repeat. This strategy turns your listening into something you can say in a discussion or write in an essay.

How to do it

  1. Identify the purpose: inform, persuade or inspire? Look for "should", "we need to" and calls to action.
  2. Map the argument: claim → reasons → evidence.
  3. Ask: Is the evidence strong? Is anything missing? Would this work in my city or country?
  4. Prepare your response: "I agree/disagree because…"

Listen for

Persuasion signals
  • We should / must / need to…
  • Imagine…
  • What if…?
  • The answer is…
  • Let's…
Your response
  • I agree that… because…
  • I'm not sure that… because…
  • The speaker's evidence for… is strong/weak because…
  • In my country,…

Tip: You can agree with the goal but disagree with the solution. Good discussions often start there.

This is also the Unit 3 capstone. In Listen 2 you will use all the Unit 3 strategies, with less help.

What if we gave 70 percent of the streets to car-free, to everybody else, so that the transit could move well for them, so that they could walk, so they could bike? Purpose: persuade (signals: *Think of the logic*, *What if…?*, *Why not…?*). Claim: give 70% of street space to walking, bikes and transit. Reasons: fairness (only ⅓ have cars) + cities work better. Show a model summary

Peter Calthorpe argues that sprawl, which he defines as any development that isolates people, is the main problem in how we build cities. He supports this with California scenarios showing that compact, walkable growth would use less land, produce less carbon and save households money, and with examples of high-density sprawl in China. His solution is seven principles, including mixed use, walking, biking and transit. His argument is persuasive because it uses clear numbers and real examples such as Los Angeles. However, many of his figures come from models and projections rather than measured results, and he says little about the cost of building transit. Overall, I agree with his principles, but I think each city needs its own evidence.

120 words. Your summary can be different. Compare the content, not the exact words.
