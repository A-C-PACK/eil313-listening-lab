Strategy 7 · Unit 2

Recognize research and evidence cues

Notice when a speaker reports a study: who did it, what they did, what they found.

What is it?

Talks about science report research. Evidence cues tell you that the speaker is giving facts from a study, not just an opinion.

Why does it help?

In academic listening, you need to know what the evidence is and how strong it is. Evidence cues also help you separate facts from opinions later (S11).

How to do it

  1. Listen for evidence cues: "studies show", "we found", "the data".
  2. Note three things: Who? (the researchers) What did they do? What did they find?
  3. Listen for how sure the speaker is: "shows" is stronger than "suggests".

Listen for

Reporting research
  • Studies show…
  • Research suggests…
  • We found that…
  • Scientists discovered…
  • In one study,…
  • We asked / measured / tested…
Results
  • The data showed…
  • X percent…
  • X was the best predictor of…
  • significantly more / less

Tip: Numbers are often the result of a study. When you hear a number, ask: what was measured?

Also use: S6 Listen for definitions and explanations · S5 Take notes with key words and symbols

Show the words (after you listen)
In all those very different contexts, one characteristic emerged as a significant predictor of success. And it wasn't social intelligence. It wasn't good looks, physical health, and it wasn't IQ. It was grit. Grit is passion and perseverance for very long-term goals.

Who? Her research team. What did they do? They studied cadets, spelling bee children, new teachers and salespeople. What did they find? "one characteristic emerged as a significant predictor of success… It was grit." Then she defines grit (S6). Evidence card: Who: Duckworth's team · Did: studied 4 groups · Found: grit = best predictor (not IQ).

Show a model summary

Angela Lee Duckworth, a former teacher and psychologist, studied who is successful and why. In very different settings, such as West Point, the National Spelling Bee and Chicago high schools, her team found that grit, which is passion and perseverance for long-term goals, was a significant predictor of success, more than IQ or talent. For example, grittier students were significantly more likely to graduate. However, she admits that scientists still don't know how to build grit, although growth mindset may help.
