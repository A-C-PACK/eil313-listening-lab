Strategy 8 · Unit 2

Use outline and Cornell notes

Organize your notes into main points and sub-points, then add questions and a summary.

What is it?

An outline shows main points on the left and details indented below them. Cornell notes add a narrow column for key words and questions, and a summary at the bottom.

Why does it help?

Organized notes show how ideas connect, so they are much easier to review and study from than a list of words.

How to do it

  1. Before you listen, divide your page: a narrow cue column on the left, notes on the right, a summary box at the bottom.
  2. While listening, write main points at the left of the notes column and indent the details under them.
  3. After listening, write key words or questions in the cue column.
  4. Write a 2–3 sentence summary at the bottom.

Listen for

Listen for structure
  • There are three ways…
  • The first… / The second…
  • Another…
  • Finally…
Cornell sections
  • Cue column: key words, questions
  • Notes column: main points + indented details
  • Summary: 2–3 sentences in your words

Tip: Signposts (S3) tell you when to start a new main point in your outline.

Also use: S3 Recognize signpost language · S10 Decode stress and connected speech

See it in this talk

Zogaj often starts a new main point with a question: "So, what did we just do?" "What is the brain?" In an outline or Cornell notes, each question becomes a heading or a cue, and the details go under it.

So, what did we just do? Well, we let our brain have fun, and when we did that we focused on the task. When we focus on the task, we tell our brain that this is important: remember this. So, first we enforce the power of remembering. What is the brain? The brain is a biological lump of neurons. It contains about 3% of our body weight, but consumes 20% of our energy intake every day.

Exam practice: beyond the words

On the final exam, many questions ask what a speaker means or why they say something. The answer is not in the words alone: think about what comes before and after.

Watch out for these wrong answers: ideas the speaker did not say; answers that are too strong (“all”, “always”, “only”); the opposite of the speaker's view; and answers with the right words but the wrong meaning.

Idriz Zogaj, a memory champion, explains that he is not a genius but learned memory techniques from a book. His technique is to connect information with fun, vivid stories and places in your mind.
