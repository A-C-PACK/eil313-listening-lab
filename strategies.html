Unit 1: Culture, Tradition & Progress

S1

Strategy 1 · Unit 1

Predict before you listen

Use the title, pictures and what you already know to guess what you will hear.

What is it?

Before you press play, you make guesses about the topic, the speaker's message and the words you might hear.

Why does it help?

Your brain understands faster when it already has an idea of what is coming. When the talk starts, you check your guesses instead of starting from zero.

How to do it

  1. Read the title and the information about the speaker.
  2. Ask yourself: What do I already know about this topic?
  3. Guess 2–3 things the speaker will talk about and some words you might hear.
  4. While you listen, check your guesses: right, wrong, or partly right?

Listen for

Look at
  • the title
  • the speaker's job and country
  • the first 30 seconds
  • the pictures and slides
Speakers often say
  • Today I want to talk about…
  • I'm going to tell you a story about…
  • My question is…

Tip: A wrong guess is still useful. You notice the difference, and that helps you remember.

S2

Strategy 2 · Unit 1

Listen for the main idea (gist)

On the first listen, follow the big message. Don't stop for every word.

What is it?

Listening for gist means understanding the topic and the speaker's main message, not every detail.

Why does it help?

If you stop on every new word, you lose the rest of the talk. Speakers repeat their main idea, so you will hear it more than once.

How to do it

  1. Turn captions off. Don't pause the video.
  2. Listen for words the speaker repeats. They often point to the main idea.
  3. Pay special attention to the beginning and the end. Speakers often state their message there.
  4. After listening, say the main idea in one sentence: "The talk is about… The speaker thinks…"

Listen for

Main-idea signals
  • What I'm saying is…
  • The point is…
  • To me,…
  • That's why…
  • So, …
Where to listen hardest
  • the first minute
  • the last minute
  • repeated words

Tip: It is normal to understand only 60–70% on the first listen. That is enough for the gist.

S3

Strategy 3 · Unit 1

Recognize signpost language

Notice the words that tell you where the talk is going.

What is it?

Signposts are words and phrases that show the organization of a talk: a new point, the next step, a list, or the end.

Why does it help?

Signposts are like road signs. If you hear "there are three ways", you know to listen for three things, and you can organize your notes.

How to do it

  1. Listen for numbers and list words: "three ways", "first", "another".
  2. When you hear a signpost, start a new line in your notes.
  3. Use the signposts to make a simple outline of the talk.

Listen for

Introducing a topic
  • Today I want to talk about…
  • Let me tell you about…
  • Here's the thing:
Listing and sequencing
  • There are three ways…
  • First… / Second… / Finally…
  • Another (thing)…
  • Then…
  • Next…
Moving on or concluding
  • Now,…
  • So,…
  • The key here is…
  • In the end,…
  • To sum up,…

Tip: Many TED speakers use a story signpost: "I was in…" or "When I was 11…". That tells you a story or example is starting.

S4

Strategy 4 · Unit 1

Listen for examples and supporting details

Tell the difference between a main point and the examples that support it.

What is it?

Speakers make a general point and then support it with examples, stories, numbers or reasons.

Why does it help?

Examples help you understand and remember the main point. If you know something is an example, you don't need to write every detail.

How to do it

  1. Listen for example signals: "for example", "like", "imagine", "when I…".
  2. Ask: What general point does this example support?
  3. In your notes, write the main point first. Then indent the example under it.

Listen for

Example signals
  • For example,…
  • For instance,…
  • like…
  • such as…
  • Imagine…
  • Take…
Story signals
  • I remember…
  • One day…
  • I was in…
  • When I was…
Back to the point
  • So,…
  • That's why…
  • This shows that…
  • The thing is,…

Tip: After a story, the speaker usually says what it means. Listen hard right after the story ends.

S5

Strategy 5 · Unit 1

Take notes with key words and symbols

Write only the important words. Use abbreviations and symbols to save time.

What is it?

Good lecture notes are not full sentences. They are key content words (nouns, verbs, numbers, names) plus symbols and abbreviations.

Why does it help?

People speak about 150 words per minute, and nobody can write that fast. Key words and symbols let you keep up and still understand your notes later.

How to do it

  1. Write nouns, main verbs, numbers and names. Skip small words (the, is, of).
  2. Use symbols: → (leads to), = (is/means), ↑ (increase), ↓ (decrease), + (and), ≠ (is not), ? (question).
  3. Use short forms: b/c (because), w/ (with), w/o (without), e.g. (example), ppl (people), gov (government).
  4. Right after listening, add anything you remember and fix words you can't read.

Listen for

Symbols
  • → leads to / causes
  • = is / means
  • ≠ is different from
  • ↑ more, increase
  • ↓ less, decrease
  • + and, also
  • vs. compared to
  • ? unclear / question
Short forms
  • b/c because
  • w/ with
  • w/o without
  • e.g. for example
  • ppl people
  • info information
  • edu education
  • gov government
  • % percent

Tip: Make your own symbols for words that come up again and again in a talk, for example "C" for culture.

Unit 2: The Human Mind, Intelligence & Memory

S6

Strategy 6 · Unit 2

Listen for definitions and explanations

Catch how a speaker explains a new term, and write the term and its meaning.

What is it?

Academic speakers often introduce a key term and then explain what it means, sometimes with an example.

Why does it help?

Key terms carry the main ideas of a talk. If you miss the definition, you can lose the whole argument.

How to do it

  1. Listen for definition signals: "is called", "means", "in other words".
  2. Write the term, then = and a short meaning in your own words.
  3. Add an example if the speaker gives one.

Listen for

Definition signals
  • X is…
  • X means…
  • This is called X.
  • What I call X…
  • By X, I mean…
  • In other words,…
  • that is,…
Explanation signals
  • What this means is…
  • Basically,…
  • Let me explain.
  • Here's how it works:

Tip: Speakers often say a key term slowly and stress it. Listen for a word that is said clearly and repeated.

S7

Strategy 7 · Unit 2

Recognize research and evidence cues

Notice when a speaker reports a study: who did it, what they did, what they found.

What is it?

Talks about science report research. Evidence cues tell you that the speaker is giving facts from a study, not just an opinion.

Why does it help?

In academic listening, you need to know what the evidence is and how strong it is. Evidence cues also help you separate facts from opinions later (S11).

How to do it

  1. Listen for evidence cues: "studies show", "we found", "the data".
  2. Note three things: Who? (the researchers) What did they do? What did they find?
  3. Listen for how sure the speaker is: "shows" is stronger than "suggests".

Listen for

Reporting research
  • Studies show…
  • Research suggests…
  • We found that…
  • Scientists discovered…
  • In one study,…
  • We asked / measured / tested…
Results
  • The data showed…
  • X percent…
  • X was the best predictor of…
  • significantly more / less

Tip: Numbers are often the result of a study. When you hear a number, ask: what was measured?

S8

Strategy 8 · Unit 2

Use outline and Cornell notes

Organize your notes into main points and sub-points, then add questions and a summary.

What is it?

An outline shows main points on the left and details indented below them. Cornell notes add a narrow column for key words and questions, and a summary at the bottom.

Why does it help?

Organized notes show how ideas connect, so they are much easier to review and study from than a list of words.

How to do it

  1. Before you listen, divide your page: a narrow cue column on the left, notes on the right, a summary box at the bottom.
  2. While listening, write main points at the left of the notes column and indent the details under them.
  3. After listening, write key words or questions in the cue column.
  4. Write a 2–3 sentence summary at the bottom.

Listen for

Listen for structure
  • There are three ways…
  • The first… / The second…
  • Another…
  • Finally…
Cornell sections
  • Cue column: key words, questions
  • Notes column: main points + indented details
  • Summary: 2–3 sentences in your words

Tip: Signposts (S3) tell you when to start a new main point in your outline.

S9

Strategy 9 · Unit 2

Follow cause and effect

Hear how one thing leads to another, and show it with arrows in your notes.

What is it?

Science talks explain processes: what causes something and what the result is.

Why does it help?

Understanding cause and effect means you understand why something happens, not just what happens. That is what exams and discussions ask about.

How to do it

  1. Listen for cause-and-effect words: "because", "so", "leads to", "as a result".
  2. In your notes, write the cause → the effect.
  3. Link several steps in a chain: A → B → C.

Listen for

Cause → effect
  • because (of)…
  • so…
  • leads to…
  • causes…
  • results in…
  • as a result,…
  • That's why…
  • if… then…
Effect ← cause
  • is caused by…
  • comes from…
  • is due to…
  • is the result of…

Tip: Not every "so" is a cause. Speakers also use "so" to start a new point (S3). Ask: does one thing make the other happen?

S10

Strategy 10 · Unit 2

Decode stress and connected speech

Hear the stressed key words, and recognize words that blend together in fast speech.

What is it?

In natural speech, important words are louder and longer (stressed), and small words become short and blend together: "want to" sounds like "wanna", "did you" like "didja".

Why does it help?

Many listening problems are not about vocabulary. You know the words, but you don't recognize them when they are spoken quickly. Training your ear on short clips fixes this.

How to do it

  1. Play a short clip. Write the stressed words first. They carry the meaning.
  2. Play it again and fill in the small words between them.
  3. Compare with the transcript. Notice which words were hard to hear and why.
  4. Slow the video to 0.75× if needed, then try again at normal speed.

Listen for

Common blends
  • going to → gonna
  • want to → wanna
  • kind of → kinda
  • did you → didja
  • a lot of → a lotta
  • them → 'em
Weak forms
  • to → /tə/
  • and → 'n
  • of → /əv/ or /ə/
  • can → /kən/
  • for → /fə/

Tip: Stressed words alone often give you the meaning: "FATHER… very HAPPY".

S11

Strategy 11 · Unit 2

Separate fact from opinion

Decide whether the speaker is reporting evidence or giving their own view, and how certain they are.

What is it?

Speakers mix facts (things that can be checked: research, numbers, events) with opinions (beliefs, judgments, recommendations). Hedging words show how certain they are.

Why does it help?

Critical listeners don't accept everything they hear. You need to know what is proven, what is suggested, and what is just the speaker's belief.

How to do it

  1. Ask: Could this be checked with evidence? If yes, it is probably a fact or a claim about a fact.
  2. Listen for opinion signals: "I think", "I believe", "should".
  3. Listen for hedges ("may", "might", "suggests") and boosters ("clearly", "proves").
  4. Mark your notes: F = fact, O = opinion.

Listen for

Opinion signals
  • I think / I believe…
  • In my view,…
  • We should / must…
  • It's wrong to…
  • I worry that…
Hedges (less sure)
  • may / might / could
  • suggests
  • it seems
  • probably
  • some people
Boosters (very sure)
  • clearly
  • proves
  • definitely
  • always / never
  • the fact is

Tip: A strong opinion can be based on facts. The question is whether the speaker gives the evidence.

Unit 3: Urban Challenges

S12

Strategy 12 · Unit 3

Follow problem–solution structure

Track the problem, its causes, and the solution. Listen for contrast words at turning points.

What is it?

Many talks about social issues follow a pattern: Here is a problem → here is why it happens → here is a solution → here are the results.

Why does it help?

If you know the pattern, you can predict what comes next and organize your notes in the same shape.

How to do it

  1. Listen for the problem: "the problem is", "the challenge".
  2. Listen for causes: "because", "this is due to".
  3. Listen for the turning point to the solution. It often starts with "but", "instead" or "what if".
  4. Take notes in three boxes: Problem / Causes / Solution (+ results).

Listen for

Problem
  • The problem is…
  • The challenge is…
  • This is a crisis.
  • We face…
Contrast and turning points
  • But…
  • However,…
  • Instead,…
  • On the other hand,…
  • What if…?
  • So we decided to…
Solution and results
  • The solution is…
  • We designed…
  • As a result,…
  • Now,…

Tip: "But" is the most important word in many talks. What comes after "but" is usually what the speaker really believes.

S13

Strategy 13 · Unit 3

Listen for numbers and comparisons

Hear numbers, percentages and comparisons correctly, and understand what they show.

What is it?

Speakers use statistics and comparisons to make a point stronger: "55% own two cars", "twice as long", "fewer deaths than before".

Why does it help?

Numbers are easy to mishear (fifteen/fifty) and easy to forget. Even if you catch the number, you also need to know what it measures and why the speaker uses it.

How to do it

  1. Write numbers as numerals immediately: 91%, $435, 1,000.
  2. Next to each number, write what it measures.
  3. Listen for comparison words: more/less than, twice, increase, drop.
  4. Ask: What point does this number support?

Listen for

Numbers
  • percent (%)
  • half / a third / a quarter
  • a million / a billion
  • fifteen vs. fifty (stress!)
Comparison
  • more / less / fewer than
  • twice as… / three times more
  • the same as
  • compared to
  • increased / dropped by
  • -er than / the most

Tip: Teen or ty? In "fifTEEN" the stress is at the end. In "FIFty" it is at the start.

S14

Strategy 14 · Unit 3

Take comparison notes (T-chart)

Organize your notes in two columns to compare before/after, problem/solution, or two options.

What is it?

A T-chart is a page with two columns. You put matching information side by side, for example the street before and after a change.

Why does it help?

When a speaker compares, a list of notes hides the comparison. Two columns show it clearly and make it easy to explain later.

How to do it

  1. Before listening, decide what is being compared and write two headings.
  2. While listening, put each detail in the correct column on the same row as the detail it matches.
  3. After listening, add a conclusion under the chart: what does the comparison show?

Listen for

Comparison signals
  • before / after
  • used to… now…
  • in contrast
  • while / whereas
  • similarly
  • instead of

Tip: Leave space. Speakers often add the matching detail for the other column a few seconds later.

S15

Strategy 15 · Unit 3

Summarize and paraphrase from notes

Use only your notes to explain the talk in your own words.

What is it?

A summary gives the speaker's main idea and key supporting points in a few sentences. Paraphrasing means saying it in your own words, not copying the speaker's sentences.

Why does it help?

Summarizing shows you really understood. In university, you summarize lectures for exams, essays and discussions.

How to do it

  1. Look only at your notes, not the transcript.
  2. Start with the speaker and the main idea: "In this talk, X argues that…"
  3. Add 2–3 key points or examples. Leave out small details.
  4. Use your own words. Change the sentence structure, not just single words.

Listen for

Summary language
  • In this talk, X argues / explains that…
  • According to X,…
  • X gives the example of…
  • X's main point is that…
  • Overall,…
Reporting verbs
  • argues
  • explains
  • describes
  • claims
  • suggests
  • shows

Tip: If you can't write the summary from your notes, the notes need improving. That is useful information too.

S16

Strategy 16 · Unit 3

Evaluate the argument and the speaker's purpose

Decide why the speaker is speaking, what they want you to believe or do, and whether the evidence supports it.

What is it?

Every talk has a purpose: to inform, to persuade or to inspire. Evaluating an argument means asking whether the reasons and evidence really support the speaker's claim.

Why does it help?

University students are expected to respond critically, not just repeat. This strategy turns your listening into something you can say in a discussion or write in an essay.

How to do it

  1. Identify the purpose: inform, persuade or inspire? Look for "should", "we need to" and calls to action.
  2. Map the argument: claim → reasons → evidence.
  3. Ask: Is the evidence strong? Is anything missing? Would this work in my city or country?
  4. Prepare your response: "I agree/disagree because…"

Listen for

Persuasion signals
  • We should / must / need to…
  • Imagine…
  • What if…?
  • The answer is…
  • Let's…
Your response
  • I agree that… because…
  • I'm not sure that… because…
  • The speaker's evidence for… is strong/weak because…
  • In my country,…

Tip: You can agree with the goal but disagree with the solution. Good discussions often start there.
